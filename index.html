For a century, neuroscience could watch the brain but not steer it. Then three researchers followed a pond alga’s taste for sunlight all the way to a protein that lets you switch a single nerve cell on with a flash of blue light.

The problem

A map with no switches

A human brain weighs about — and holds — nerve cells, each wired to thousands of others. Through the twentieth century researchers worked out roughly which regions did what: this patch for movement, that one for speech.

But the tools were blunt. You could damage a region and see what broke, or record from it and see what lit up — and neither proves that these cells cause that feeling. Cells doing completely different jobs sit tangled together; a single neuron may reach clear across the brain. The picture that emerged was, in the Nobel Committee’s phrase, a blurry photograph.

Francis Crick, who had moved from DNA to consciousness, put his finger on the gap in 1979: neuroscience needed a way to control one kind of neuron while leaving every other cell alone. Two decades later he suggested what the signal should be — light, because light is fast enough to keep up with a nerve impulse. He conceded the idea sounded far-fetched.

It was already swimming around in a pond.

Act I · 1980s–1992 · Peter Hegemann

The alga that swims towards the light

Chlamydomonas is a single-celled green alga with two whip-like flagella and one piece of equipment that matters here: an eyespot, a speck of orange on the side of the cell containing the light-catching molecule retinal.

Stir the algae into a dish and the water turns faintly green. Shine a light on one side and the green moves — millions of cells swimming toward the lamp.

Try it: click or tap anywhere in the dish to move the lamp.

How fast is the alga, really?

Hegemann put microelectrodes on the cell and measured the electrical response to a flash. It arrived after about —. For comparison, light hitting a human eye sets off a chain of chemical steps that takes — — more than twenty times longer.

That speed was the clue. A long chemical relay cannot run in half a millisecond. Hegemann’s conclusion, in the early 1990s, was that the alga must be doing it with one protein that both catches the photon and opens as an ion channel.

No such protein was known. Colleagues were sceptical. Isolating it from the eyespot failed for years — pulled out of the cell, the thing fell apart.

Act II · 2002–2003 · Georg Nagel & Peter Hegemann

One protein, two jobs

Around 2000 a Japanese team published the Chlamydomonas genome. Hegemann’s group combed it and found two genes that looked like light-catching proteins. Which one — if either — was the channel?

Hegemann sent them to Georg Nagel in Frankfurt, who had the right trick: put the gene into a frog’s egg cell. The oocyte is enormous, easy to impale with an electrode, and will obligingly manufacture whatever protein you give it and park it in its membrane.

Nagel injected the genes, waited, and shone light on the eggs. Both proteins were ion channels that opened in the light. The decade-old hypothesis was right. They named them channelrhodopsin-1 and channelrhodopsin-2.

Channelrhodopsin-2 was the one that mattered. It opened within — of the light arriving, and it let positive ions pour in — enough to make a clean electrical signal. Then came the test that decided everything: they put the gene into human kidney cells, and into hamster cells. Those cells became light-sensitive too.

In their 2003 paper, Nagel and Hegemann wrote that channelrhodopsin-2 could be used as a tool to generate electrical impulses in cells using light. The sentence was an invitation.

Try it: drag the wavelength slider. Only blue light opens the channel — which is why green and red leave it alone, and why experiments later used other colours to drive other opsins independently.

Act III · 2005 · Karl Deisseroth

The neuron obeys

Karl Deisseroth had trained as a psychiatrist as well as a scientist. In the clinic he had watched patients whose treatments barely worked, and concluded that the only way forward was to understand what neurons were actually doing in a living brain. When he heard about channelrhodopsin-2 he wrote to Nagel and asked for the DNA.

He put it into cultured rat neurons — worried the foreign gene would poison these notoriously delicate cells. It did not. He flashed blue light. The neurons fired, on command, on the millisecond timescale, and passed the signal on to their neighbours. That was 2005.

Three ways this goes wrong — all of them real

Not enough opsin

Below roughly a fifth of the default expression the photocurrent never reaches threshold. The light is on, the channel opens, and the cell simply sits there. Slide opsin down to see it.

The channel runs down

Channelrhodopsin-2 does not reset instantly. After a flash, part of the protein population sits in a light-adapted closed state that takes seconds to recover. Drive it fast and each pulse has fewer channels available than the last — watch the photocurrent trace shrink along the train.

The cell gets stuck

Past a certain rate the leftover current never fully decays between pulses. The membrane stays slightly depolarised, sodium channels stay inactivated, and the neuron falls into depolarisation block: it stops firing altogether rather than firing faster.

That ceiling is why the field kept engineering new opsins after 2005 — faster variants that recover sooner, and others that silence neurons instead of exciting them.

Act IV · 2007 · into a living brain

Light in, whiskers out

A neuron in a dish is a long way from a brain. The next step took two more years: deliver the channelrhodopsin-2 gene to one cell type in the motor cortex of a living mouse, then get light to it through a thin optical fibre threaded through a small hole in the skull.

Light in. Whiskers move. The experiment is almost comically simple and it settled the question Crick had posed: these cells, and not their neighbours, drive this movement.

The same year, the method woke sleeping mice on cue by switching on a cell type suspected of controlling wakefulness. In 2012, working with Susumu Tonegawa, Deisseroth reactivated an engram — the specific pattern of cells laid down when a mouse learned to fear a place. Light on, and the mouse was afraid of nothing at all. It was the first demonstration of exactly which neurons a particular memory needs.

Try it: the fibre below carries whatever pulse train you set in Act III. Push the rate past the cell’s ceiling and the whiskers stop moving, too.

      And beyond the brain

      In people

      The first clinical results are in. In an ongoing trial, a channelrhodopsin-like protein was placed in the retina of a person blinded by retinitis pigmentosa, a disease that destroys the rods and cones. Wearing light-emitting goggles, they could make out objects on a table and pick them up.

      There is hope, too, for cochlear implants: today they stimulate the auditory nerve with electricity, which spreads. Light would not.
